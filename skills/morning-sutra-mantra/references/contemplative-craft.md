# Contemplative craft

## Work with the verse, not around it

Before composing, privately write a one-sentence account of what the verse says in its context. Then identify what your application adds. If the modern lesson could remain unchanged under any other verse, it is too generic.

For a term with several readings, preserve the useful ambiguity. “Practice” might refer to a sustained discipline, not merely today's posture class. A claim about stillness is not automatically a claim that thoughts must disappear. Let context and a reliable commentary constrain interpretation. Do not use a metaphor about the nervous system as an established physiological mechanism.

When the verse concerns metaphysics or extraordinary capacities, explain that context rather than turning it into a promise of health, control, or supernatural ability. An assistant can invite contemplation without presenting a tradition's metaphysical claim as an empirical fact.

## One image must do real work

Choose a concrete sensation or ordinary action that changes meaning as the paragraph unfolds. The image should open attention, not illustrate a prewritten self-help rule. Possible fictional starting points: keeping a foot steady while reaching; noticing an urge to fill a pause in conversation; returning to a difficult task without clenching the jaw. These are invented examples, not assumptions about the reader.

Bring the body into the sentence through observable action. “Feel the ground through the standing foot” is different from “raise your vibration.” Avoid assigning emotions to body parts or telling a reader that an uncomfortable sensation proves stored trauma is being released.

## Relationship without mind-reading

Write about the reader's possible response, not another person's presumed motives. “Leave room to hear the end of the sentence” can be an invitation; “they are withdrawing because they are afraid” is an invented explanation. Do not use spiritual language to erase a boundary, justify mistreatment, or make reconciliation obligatory.

## Depth without bloat

A useful reflection contains a small turn: the effort is still there, but its quality changes; a reaction is noticed before it becomes action; a pause remains unfilled. Do not spell out the moral after the image has already carried it. Remove any sentence that merely paraphrases the previous one.

A mantra is a portable practice, not an assertion the user must believe. Original examples, for form only:

- “I can return without beginning again.”
- “Let the next word have room.”
- “Steady enough to soften.”

Do not reuse these as a default bank. Do not imply they are translations of a cited sutra.

## Form variation

Alternate between spare prose, short lines, a sustained image, or a quieter question. Keep the verse identifiable and the meaning readable. Do not introduce novelty for its own sake. Track the opening image, whether the mantra uses breath, and the closing form so variation can be checked rather than assumed.

If writing for an unfamiliar reader, avoid faux intimacy. A clear short piece is better than a detailed invented life. If the user asks for greater depth, develop the verse's conceptual tension and embodied application; do not simply double the adjective count.
